Spavin, the ankylosis of tarsal joints, is one of the most frequently described palaeopathologies in cattle. Although spavin is related to multiple risk factors, it is traditionally perceived as being caused by traction. This lesion is well-studied in in vivo animals; however, comparative data from documented cattle skeletal collections is unavailable. A review of literature from British archaeological sites shows that spavin in cattle is only documented after the Iron Age. By analysing 127 modern non-draught cattle kept under similar conditions, this study demonstrates that spavin strongly correlates with age and body weight. The prevalence rate of spavin in the studied sample is c.20%, with no observed correlation with sex. Restricted movement could be an additional factor contributing to the elevated prevalence of spavin in the modern non-draught sample. These factors may partly explain the higher frequency of spavin observed in historic times compared to prehistory, reflecting changes in livestock management. The potential influence of traction on spavin should not be ignored, but this paper argues that the connection between spavin and draught use is primarily mediated by age and other factors.
